April 1993. San Jose, California.
Jensen Huang co-founds Nvidia in a Denny's booth.
He runs the company for thirty-three years.

[warm, nostalgic] “Three engineers, one booth — and bottomless coffee.”
[small child voice, curious] “Did you have a business plan?”
[dry, amused] “We had an idea. And… a LOT of refills.”
[small child voice, whiny] “And the leather jacket?”
[warm, outgoing] “The jacket came later. The coffee came first.”
[small child voice, flat] “So… I was born in a diner.”
[chuckles] “The best ones usually are.”

His original goal?
Make video games look less like… wet cardboard.
Standard computer chips work like one genius professor.
Brilliant.
Very serious.
But he solves just ONE math problem at a time.

Nvidia's chip works differently.
Picture ten thousand kindergarteners holding crayons.
None of them knows calculus…
[deadpan] Some eat the paste.
But they ALL color at the exact same moment.
In tech speak, that's a GPU.

A factory for tiny math, done all at once.
[lower, conspiratorial] Then AI researchers notice a secret.

What is AI training?
TRILLIONS of tiny math problems.
Exactly what those kindergarteners do best.

[small child voice, whiny] “I used to draw dragons for video games.”
[small child voice, flat and bossy] “Now you do math. Keep coloring.”
[small child voice, whiny] “How many problems are left?”
[small child voice, deadpan] “A few trillion… give or take.”
[small child voice, whiny, close to tears] “My crayons are melting!”
[small child voice, deadpan] “That means it's working. Welcome to AI.”

Suddenly, every tech giant needs Nvidia's flagship chips, like the H100.
Each chip costs tens of THOUSANDS of dollars.
Does it come with a free game?
[deadpan] Not a chance.
