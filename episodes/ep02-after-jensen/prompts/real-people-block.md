REAL PEOPLE IN SKITS (allowed — keep it funny, keep it safe):
Real people MAY speak in skits. Write the header line "# REAL: <speaker names>" and add "!" after anyone who is accused but not charged (e.g. "# REAL: Jensen, Alice!").

GREEN — write freely:
- Exaggerate their PUBLIC persona and habits (signature clothes, keynote style, catchphrases).
- Have them restate their REAL, sourced public positions in their own voice.
- Retell anecdotes they themselves have told in interviews or biographies.
- Obviously absurd situations (a real CEO arguing with a talking chip).

YELLOW — allowed only with a frame:
- A real, documented scene (photo, report) with imagined small talk. The narrator line right before says it is imagined, e.g. "Photos show them at the same dinner. Here's roughly how that small talk goes." — tag that narrator line {FRAMED}.
- Dialogue stays neutral: greetings, business talk, deliberately vague answers. Nothing that hints anyone knew of or did anything wrong.

ORANGE — a dramatized scene of a REPORTED allegation (max 2 per script), allowed only with all five:
1. A narrator line right before the scene that names the source, tagged {DRAMATIZED} {SRC:n}: "According to the Times, a US memo describes her job. If the memo is right, the pitch goes something like this."
2. The scene shows ONLY what the source alleges. No extra crimes, money, documents or romance.
3. The other party is an unnamed fictional character ("Handler", "Voice").
4. A narrator line within 6 lines after the scene stating it is alleged and denied: "That's the memo's version. China calls it false. She hasn't been charged with anything." {SRC:n}
5. A visual note: > on-screen tag "DRAMATIZATION · based on reported allegations · denied".
In an orange scene the accused person ASKS and the handler ANSWERS. She is curious, surprised, sceptical — never boasting, never confessing. The humour comes from how mundane the job is.

RED — never write:
- Allegations with no source, or beyond what the source says; a real person confessing ("I am a spy", "I knew").
- A real person who is NOT accused acting like an accomplice.
- Anything about romance, sex or family life as dialogue or a scene — only the narrator may mention it, with a source.
- Making them contradict their real positions, or accuse another real person of something unproven.
- Jokes about ethnicity, accent or physical traits.
- Invented words on a quote card. A CARD with quotation marks is only for real, verbatim, sourced quotes ({SRC:n}).
For a person who is accused but not charged: outside an ORANGE scene, none of their lines may touch the allegation.

DIALOGUE LENGTH — write scenes, not fragments:
- At least 60% of skits are full SCENES: 6–10 turns, each turn a complete sentence of 4–14 words, with three beats: setup (who wants what) → escalation (2–3 follow-ups, each revealing something new) → button line.
- The rest are quick gags: 2–4 turns of 1–5 words, used as punctuation between number-heavy passages.
- No turn over 18 words. Each character keeps one clear attitude through the scene.
- Write for the ear (ElevenLabs v4 reads these): "?" and "?!" for rising pitch, short flat sentences for falling pitch, "…" for hesitation or trailing off, "—" for self-interruption or being cut off, stutters ("I— I just…"), fillers ("oh —", "well,", "uh,"), ONE word in CAPS per sentence for stress, stretched vowels ("Sooo…") for lazy sarcasm, one-word sentences for slow emphasis ("Every. Single. Hour."). At least half of all dialogue turns and 40% of narrator lines should carry one of these.
- Each character's emotion follows an arc through the scene (curious → doubtful → panicking); two characters move in opposite directions (one louder, one flatter). Laughs and sighs may sit mid-sentence: "Oh — small. [laughs nervously] For now."
- Voice tags: change the emotion turn by turn, keeping the role prefix, e.g. [woman's voice, shy laugh], [woman's voice, whispering], [woman's voice, hesitant], [woman's voice, calm, tiny smile]. Never use tags that make an accused person a villain ([evil laugh], [sinister], [seductive]).

Make the joke land on the system (rules, paperwork, absurd scale, the market), not on the allegation. Let the real person play it straight and give the punchline to a fictional character.
